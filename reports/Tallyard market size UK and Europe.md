# Subscriptions, not commission, will carry Tallyard

Tallyard's realistically obtainable market is about **£0.5m to £2.1m a year of revenue in the UK by 2030**, plus **£0.15m to £0.6m in continental Europe** if it opens one city there by 2029. Roughly 70% of that comes from subscriptions, not commission. The reason is the size of the materials trade. Reclaimed construction materials sell for only a few hundred million pounds a year in each market: **about £0.3bn in the UK (defensible range £0.1bn to £0.6bn) and roughly €0.3bn to €0.9bn across the EU and EFTA**. An 8% commission on the whole UK trade would come to about £24m, roughly the same as the **£15m to £25m a year** that engaged UK firms could pay in subscriptions at Tallyard's placeholder prices. Counting from the bottom up, central London's office strip-outs and demolitions hold about **£10m to £20m a year of saleable material** in Tallyard's families, which is under £2m a year at 8%. By contrast, the theoretical UK subscription ceiling is £120m to £140m, and Europe's architects alone are worth about £0.7bn in seats. The numbers move with carbon policy, not waste policy. Recovery targets are already met by crushing, so reuse demand depends on the draft new London Plan's upfront carbon targets, the City of London and Westminster retrofit-first tests, the EU's 2028 whole-life carbon disclosure and France's mandatory pre-demolition resource audit. Comparable companies point the same way. No reuse-only platform in Europe has passed about €5m of revenue. France's best-backed reuse marketplace, Cycle Up, went into receivership in February 2026. The one venture-scale company in the field, One Click LCA (€20.1m revenue in 2024), sells carbon compliance software. Every figure here comes from search-result extracts, not full documents. The load-bearing ones (Salvo's £300m, the £248 a tonne realised resale value, UKGBC's 4% reuse rate, ADEME's 240,000 tonnes and Future Market Insights' USD 1.0bn) need checking against primary sources before anyone relies on them.

## The realistic prize is £0.5m to £2.1m a year by 2030

The report sizes each revenue line in three layers. **TAM** is the whole reclaimed construction materials trade by value (gross merchandise value, GMV) in each geography. For subscriptions, it is every organisation and professional who could in principle subscribe at Tallyard's placeholder prices (supplier organisation £4,800, buyer organisation £3,600, professional seat £1,200 a year). **SAM** is the part that Tallyard's model serves. On the materials side, that means commercial donor buildings yielding Tallyard's families (structural steel, raised access floor, stone and brick cladding, timber, glazing), in central London today and in other UK cities by 2030. On the subscription side, it means firms already engaged with carbon and circularity. **SOM** is what a London-first entrant can realistically win in its launch year (2026 to 2027) and by about 2030.

Materials revenue here is the 8% commission plus an **assumed 1 to 3 percentage points of GMV** from storage brokerage, testing referral and transport margin. No reuse broker publishes these rates. The assumption rests on SCI's estimate that testing (£53 a tonne) and transport and storage (£34 a tonne) add about £87 a tonne to reused steel, roughly a tenth of its reclaimed value ([SCI Report 1 via ASBP](https://asbp.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Barriers-to-structural-steel-reuse-and-means-of-overcoming-them-SCI-Report-1.pdf)). "Europe" means the EU27 plus EFTA, excluding the UK. Euro amounts are converted at €1 = £0.86, a rate the researchers did not verify.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| TAM, materials GMV | £0.3bn (range £0.1bn to £0.6bn) | £0.3bn to £0.5bn (policy upside £1.4bn) | £0.26bn to £0.76bn (€0.3bn to €0.9bn) | £0.3bn to £1.4bn |
| TAM, commission pool at 8% | £24m (range £8m to £48m) | £24m to £40m | £21m to £61m | £24m to £110m |

| SAM, materials GMV | £9m to £20m (central London offices) | £15m to £35m (London plus other UK cities) | £20m to £60m (assumption) | £30m to £100m (assumption) |
| SAM, commission at 8% | £0.7m to £1.6m | £1.2m to £2.8m | £1.6m to £4.8m | £2.4m to £8m |
| SAM, subscriptions | £15m to £25m | £15m to £25m (over £30m with national carbon rules) | £15m to £45m in regulation-led markets | widening after the EPBD's 2028 disclosure |
| SOM, materials GMV | £0.2m to £1m | £1.5m to £6m | nil | £0.5m to £2m |
| SOM, materials revenue | £20k to £0.1m | £0.14m to £0.66m | nil | £45k to £0.22m |
| SOM, subscriptions | £45k to £0.13m | £0.4m to £1.4m | nil | £0.1m to £0.4m |
| **SOM, total revenue** | **£0.06m to £0.24m** | **£0.5m to £2.1m** | **nil** | **£0.15m to £0.6m** |

The table shows three things. First, unit values cap the commission line. Reclaimed goods are cheap, and the discount to new is thin, so even capturing every serviceable London donor building at 8% yields under £2m a year today. Second, at the midpoints the UK subscription SAM is ten to twenty times the commission SAM. Even the commission on the entire UK reclaimed trade (£24m) would only equal what engaged firms could pay in subscriptions. Third, Europe adds little before 2030. Its trade is larger in total but spread across markets with different rules, and only France has the mandatory audit and reuse reporting that would let a London-built model travel. The sections that follow show how each cell was built and where the two methods disagree.

## Reclaimed materials trade a few hundred million pounds a year in each market

### The UK figure swings sixfold depending on what counts as reuse

Three top-down anchors exist for the UK, and none was built for this purpose. The most quoted is Salvo founder Thornton Kay's estimate that architectural salvage sells for **about £300m a year**, about 1% of building materials spend and "barely changed since the 1990s" ([Decomag](https://decomag.co.uk/content/salvage-and-reclamation-its-womans-world)). That is an interview estimate. The last full trade surveys date from 1998 and 2007. They showed sales value rising while salvaged volumes fell, as dealers sold more new and reproduction stock ([Building Conservation](https://www.buildingconservation.com/articles/salvage-sustainability/salvage-sustainability.htm)). Most of the £300m is heritage and domestic product sold through yards. Commercial strip-out materials such as steel sections and raised access floor move through stockholders and specialist resellers instead.

A second anchor multiplies three figures: UKGBC's estimate that **about 4%** of building materials are directly reused ([UKGBC](https://ukgbc.org/resources/reused-materials-exchange-marketplace/)), England's 63.3 Mt of non-hazardous construction and demolition waste in 2024 ([Agg-Net reporting Defra](https://www.agg-net.com/news/construction-waste-recovery-rate-reaches-953)), and the one realised resale value found, about £248 a tonne. The product is **about £0.6bn**. But it applies a share of materials to a tonnage of waste, so read it as an order of magnitude only. A third anchor runs the other way. Applying France's measured reuse intensity (below) to a UK construction market of similar size gives only **about £0.1bn**.

Recovery statistics do not separate reuse from crushing. England recovers 95.3% of its construction and demolition waste, but nearly all of that becomes aggregate. Reuse estimates for structural steel range from 5% to 7% in SCI's demolition contractor survey to 13% in a UKGBC event summary ([SteelConstruction.info](https://steelconstruction.info/topics/sustainability/the-recycling-and-reuse-survey/); [UKGBC Circular Steel](https://ukgbc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Circular-Steel-Event-Wrap-Up.pdf)). The long-run trend is stagnation. ReLondon reports that reuse tonnage fell 25% between 1998 and 2007 ([ReLondon](https://relondon.gov.uk/resources/report-re-usable-buildings-an-exploration-of-the-business-case)), and Salvo's value has been flat for three decades.

This report takes **£0.3bn as the central UK TAM**, a commission pool of about £24m. The construction products industry turns over more than **£68bn a year** ([CPA](https://constructionproducts.org.uk/media/lgwkubof/cpa-representing-the-construction-products-sector-2026-v2.pdf)), so each percentage point of product spend switched to reclaimed goods is worth about £0.68bn. Between Salvo's flat history and the 12.2% a year forecast by one vendor report, the UK trade reaches £0.3bn to £0.5bn by 2030. The £1.4bn upside case is what 2% of product spend would mean. That is the UK average for brought-in reclaimed material that Salvo's 1990s Reclamation Protocol used ([SalvoWEB](https://www.salvoweb.com/salvonews/41763-making-the-case-for-more-reuse)), and also France's 2024 reuse target ([Valobat](https://novabuild.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/VALOBAT-Marine-Webinaire-Novabuild.pdf)). Reaching it would take binding carbon limits, which the UK does not yet have.

### Europe trades well under €1bn, and vendor reports disagree by a factor of twenty

Construction produced 38.4% of the EU's 2,233 Mt of waste in 2022 ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Waste_statistics)). Mineral waste from construction and demolition, excluding soil, runs at about 370 Mt a year ([EEA briefing via Energy in Demand](https://energyindemand.com/2020/01/17/new-eea-briefing-on-construction-and-demolition-waste/)). The EU's 70% recovery target was met long ago, with about 89% of the mineral fraction recovered in 2022, but mostly as backfilling and road sub-base ([JRC](https://www.shareyourgreendesign.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/TEXT.pdf)). Only **about 1% of building elements** are reused after first use in North-West Europe ([Interreg FCRBE](https://vb.nweurope.eu/media/16598/statistical-analysis-v15.pdf)).

The best bottom-up evidence is French. ADEME counts **240,000 t of building products reused in 2024**, under 1% of the market. The sector runs through 385 sites and 10 digital players, with about 1,600 full-time equivalent (FTE) staff ([ADEME](https://infos.ademe.fr/economie-circulaire-dechets/2026/reemploi-etat-des-lieux-dun-secteur-qui-se-structure/)). No euro value has been published. At RotorDC's turnover of about €83,000 per FTE (€1.6m over 19.3 FTE) ([New European Bauhaus prizes](https://prizes.new-european-bauhaus.europa.eu/past-applications/rotor-deconstruction)), the French sector turns over roughly **€80m to €130m**. France is about 14% of EU27 construction investment (€258bn of €1,821bn in 2024) ([FIEC](https://pedmede.gr/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/FIEC_KEY_FIGURES_2025_e-leaflet.en_-1.pdf)). EU-wide reuse at French intensity would therefore be €0.55bn to €0.95bn. Because France leads on policy, actual EU trade is probably **€0.3bn to €0.7bn**.

The closest-scoped vendor estimate is Future Market Insights' **USD 1.0bn** for European component reuse in 2025, rising to USD 3.8bn by 2036 at 12.2% a year ([Future Market Insights](https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/prefabricated-building-element-deconstruction-and-component-reuse-industry-in-europe)). It sits at the top of the bottom-up range and implies about USD 1.8bn (£1.35bn) by 2030, but it discloses no method. Reclaimed lumber reports should be discarded. They put the global market at USD 62bn to 64bn, with Europe at 28% to 40% ([Grand View Research](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/reclaimed-lumber-market); [Fortune Business Insights](https://www.fortunebusinessinsights.com/reclaimed-lumber-market-117161)). That is twenty to fifty times the French-based estimate, so it must count recycled wood products. If France's 5% reuse target applied across the EU, ten to twenty times today's rate, the theoretical ceiling would be €5bn to €19bn. That is a bound, not a forecast.

The UK's £0.3bn and Europe's €0.3bn to €0.9bn look inconsistent, since Europe's construction market is about seven times the UK's: €1,821bn (about £1,566bn) against a UK total of about £234bn, an IBISWorld estimate that the ONS has not confirmed ([IBISWorld](https://script.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/bed/total-value-of-construction/44139); [ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/constructionindustry/articles/constructionstatistics/2024/pdf)). The gap comes from definitions. The UK figure includes heritage salvage and dealer retail, while the European one covers building-product reuse. On a like-for-like basis the UK trade is nearer £0.1bn to £0.3bn, and Europe including the UK is roughly £0.4bn to £1.1bn.

## Central London donor buildings hold £10m to £20m of saleable material a year

Deloitte's crane survey gives the cleanest donor proxy. Central London office starts were **4.8m sq ft (about 446,000 m2) across 57 schemes in 2025**: 3.1m sq ft of refurbishment and 1.6m sq ft of new build. That compares with 7.5m sq ft in 2024, 8.7m sq ft in 2023 and a five-year average of 6.5m sq ft (about 604,000 m2) ([Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/uk/en/about/press-room/deloitte-london-office-crane-survey-april-2026.html); [Specfinish](https://specfinish.co.uk/deloitte-london-office-crane-survey/)). Nearly every refurbishment strips the building to its frame, and most new-build starts replace a demolished or deep-stripped building. So roughly 450,000 to 800,000 m2 of office floor a year passes through strip-out or demolition in central London alone. The pipeline is long. Knight Frank counts **97.6m sq ft of London offices (38% of stock) below EPC C** and 168.8m sq ft below the proposed 2030 EPC B threshold ([Knight Frank](https://www.knightfrank.co.uk/research/article/2024/5/insight-6-retrofit-or-repurpose)).

Converting floor area to value rests on four thin data points. For the fit-out layer, UK office fit-outs produce about 77 kg of waste per m2 per cycle ([Piadeh et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1108/ECAM-05-2025-0742)), and on one London strip-out Material Index sold 68.43 t across eight reuse partners for just over £17,000, about £248 a tonne ([UKGBC](https://ukgbc.org/resources/reused-materials-exchange-marketplace/)); at that price the fit-out layer is worth about **£19 per m2** if all of it sells. Steel-framed offices hold 55 to 110 kg of primary steel per m2 ([SteelConstruction.info](https://steelconstruction.info/topics/cost-of-structural-steelwork/cost-planning-multi-storey-offices)), and reclaimed steel has sold at about £850 a tonne against about £2,200 for new fabricated steel ([ASBP case study](https://asbp.org.uk/case-studies/steel-reuse-domestic-refurbishment-project)). Raised access floor is the most liquid family, but its discount is thin: reclaimed panels trade at about £21 to £38 per m2 against £25 to £70 new ([Access Floors Distribution](https://accessfloorsdistribution.co.uk/product/recycled-raised-access-floor-panels-31mm/); [Material Index](https://material-index.co.uk/categories/raised-access-flooring); [CCL Interiors](https://ccl-interiors.com/blog/how-much-does-raised-access-flooring-cost-in-the-uk/)). Brick and stone sell on character and carbon rather than price. Reclaimed London stock bricks fetch £1,050 to £1,800 per thousand ([Brickseeker](https://www.brickseeker.co.uk/blog/how-much-do-bricks-cost-uk-2026); [Imperial Bricks](https://www.imperialbricks.co.uk/guidance/cost-of-bricks-in-the-uk-2025/)), and single facade lots are large, such as 550 t of Portland stone at Grosvenor's 56 Davies Street ([Grosvenor](https://www.grosvenor.com/news-insights/one-of-london%E2%80%99s-most-sustainable-office-developments-gets-approval)).

Combined, the fit-out layer on 446,000 to 808,000 m2 is worth £8.5m to £15.4m a year. New-build starts of 149,000 to 334,000 m2 imply 8,200 to 36,800 t of demolished steel. At today's 7% to 13% reuse share that is £0.5m to £4m; at full reuse it would be £7m to £31m. That gives a **central London SAM of £9m to £20m today**.

By 2030, suppose starts return to 600,000 to 800,000 m2 and steel reuse rises to between 13% and 30% under carbon targets. The London core would then be £12m to £25m. Scaling that to other UK cities, using London's 79% share of the leading fit-out contractor's income ([Construction Wave on Overbury](https://constructionwave.co.uk/2026/03/09/overbury-enjoys-significant-growth-in-london-fitout-sector/)), gives a **UK SAM of £15m to £35m**.

The top-down and bottom-up figures reconcile once their scopes are lined up. Mace estimates that circular construction could add **£1.25bn of materials to London's supply chain over a decade**, about £125m a year ([Mace](https://view.ceros.com/mace/circularity-report-page); [Development Finance Today](https://developmentfinancetoday.co.uk/article-desc-9761_London%20is%20the%20ideal%20place%20to%20build%20the%20world%E2%80%99s%20first%20circular%20construction%20economy,%20reports%20Mace)). The central London office flow is 7% to 16% of that. The gap is outer London, retail, hotels and housing, plus tenant Cat B churn, plus Mace's wider definition of circularity. UK office fit-out alone is about £2.5bn a year, a quarter of a £9.85bn fit-out market ([Barbour ABI](https://barbour-abi.com/public-sector-spend-sustainability-push-uk-fit-out-refurbishment-market-9-85bn/)), and each new fit-out strips out the last one. Steel cross-checks cleanly too. Cleveland Steel and Tubes has been the only active UK supplier of reused steel since EMR left the market in early 2025 ([HTS](https://hts.uk.com/news-views/the-future-of-steel-reuse/)), and it holds about 6,000 to 7,000 t of sections ([Circular Steel](https://circularsteel.com/sponsors/cleveland-steel-tubes/)). An ASBP-linked estimate puts the whole UK steel reuse market at £12.5m a year if barriers were removed ([PBC Today](https://www.pbctoday.co.uk/news/plant-equipment-supplies-news/significance-steel-re-use-2/20631/)). The London steel component of £0.5m to £4m sits comfortably inside that.

The economics explain why the pool is small. Over 2000 to 2016, new steel sections cost on average only £280 to £313 a tonne more than scrap. Reuse added about £368 a tonne: deconstruction £161, extra fabrication £120, transport and storage £34, testing £53 (2016 prices) ([SCI Report 1](https://asbp.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/Barriers-to-structural-steel-reuse-and-means-of-overcoming-them-SCI-Report-1.pdf)). Reuse therefore clears only with a carbon premium or a nearby receiving site. Tallyard's storage, testing and transport lines target exactly that gap.

Per deal, the money concentrates in steel and stone. 30 Duke Street St James's reclaimed about 440 t, 78% of its frame, and its developer GPE returned another 400 t to the market ([Build News](https://www.buildnews.co.uk/30-duke-street-tops-out-as-londons-largest-commercial-reused-steel-project/); [DirectorsTalk](https://www.directorstalkinterviews.com/great-portland-estates-completes-fully-pre-let-30-duke-street-st-james-s/4121261673)). A 440 t package at £700 to £1,000 a tonne earns £25,000 to £35,000 at 8%. Material Index's £17,000 showcase earns £1,360.

The materials SOM assumes **20 to 40 donor projects a year by 2030** (whole buildings and tenant strip-outs) averaging £75,000 to £150,000 of sold material. That gives **£1.5m to £6m of GMV**, about a tenth to a sixth of the UK SAM at matching ends of each range, and £0.14m to £0.66m of revenue. The project count is plausible against the GLA's 71 Circular Economy Statements for 93 referable cases in 2023 ([GLA](https://www.london.gov.uk/media/107727/download)), Material Index's 70 or more UK projects ([Stone Specialist](https://stonespecialist.com/news/profile-material-index)) and Backacia's 600 source sites in France in 2021 ([motherbase](https://beta.motherbase.ai/startup/10664-backacia)).

The take rate is the biggest sensitivity. Backacia's default commission is **30% of the ex-VAT sale** ([Backacia terms](https://backacia.com/pages/cgv-cgu)), nearly four times Tallyard's 8%. Loopfront's revenue is about 4% to 5% of the €16m of material its customers manage ([Loopfront](https://blog.loopfront.com/blog/loopfront-customers-manage-material-with-a-combined-value-of-around-16-million); [Proff.no](https://www.proff.no/selskap/loopfront-as/trondheim/postordre-internetthandel/IF8MC0C10K9)). At 15%, materials revenue nearly doubles.

## Subscriptions offer a pool ten times larger than commission

The UK subscription TAM comes from multiplying sourced headcounts by the placeholder prices. The counts overlap, and many of the firms are micro businesses, so treat the total as a ceiling.

|---|---|---|---|

| Architecture businesses, as buyer organisations | about 16,066 (IBISWorld estimate) ([IBISWorld](https://style.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/industry/architectural-activities/3925)) | £3,600 | £57.8m |
| Developers with £2m to £20m turnover | 3,255 ([ONS FOI](https://cy.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/freedomofinformationfoi/turnoverofcompanies)) | £3,600 | £11.7m |
| Demolition businesses, as suppliers | 789 to 815 ([IBISWorld](https://www.ibisworld.com/united-kingdom/number-of-businesses/demolition/2480)) | £4,800 | £3.8m |

| Surveyors on audits (illustrative 2% of 100,000 UK RICS members) ([Property Industry Eye](https://propertyindustryeye.com/rics-membership-passes-100000-with-record-growth/)) | about 2,000 | £1,200 | £2.4m |

The total is **£120m to £140m a year**. The SAM narrows this to firms already engaged with carbon and circularity: 3,600 to 4,000 RIBA Chartered Practices as buyer organisations, worth £13.1m ([Building, RIBA benchmarking](https://building.co.uk/download?ac=1917910); [Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2021/07/19/riba-net-zero-carbon-challenge/)); about 500 institutional owners and developers, worth £1.8m, drawn from INREV's 145 UK members, the 33 Better Buildings Partnership signatories with over £380bn under management and the developers behind GLA referable schemes ([INREV](https://www.inrev.org/system/files/2025-05/INREV%20AGM%20presentation.pdf); [AREF on BBP](https://www.aref.org.uk/resource/the-better-buildings-partnership-bbp.html)); 140 to 156 NFDC demolition contractors as suppliers, worth £0.7m ([Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/National%20Federation%20of%20Demolition%20Contractors)); and UKGBC's membership, worth £2.5m with overlap. With seats added, the UK subscription SAM is **£15m to £25m a year**.

A bottom-up check against London policy shows how much of that is forced rather than voluntary. Each GLA referable scheme brings a team: a donor owner and a deconstruction contractor as suppliers, an architect and often a separate developer as buyers, and a surveyor and a sustainability consultant as seats. A full team costs £15,600 to £19,200 a year at Tallyard's prices. Across the 70 to 95 referable schemes a year that file Circular Economy Statements, that is **£1.1m to £1.8m**, before allowing for firms that appear on several schemes. Policy alone forces a London subscription pool of only a twentieth to an eighth of the SAM. The rest depends on voluntary adoption driven by client targets and the voluntary UK Net Zero Carbon Buildings Standard, or on national rules such as Part Z. Part Z would require whole-life carbon assessment for buildings over about 1,000 m2 or 10 dwellings ([Designing Buildings](https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/The_Net_Zero_Carbon_Buildings_Standard_and_Part_Z_explained)) and would push the SAM past £30m.

The subscription SOM of **£0.4m to £1.4m by 2030** assumes 50 to 150 buyer organisations, 20 to 60 supplier organisations and 100 to 500 seats. That is 2% to 9% of the SAM, which brackets the observed early-adopter rates: 230 of about 4,000 chartered practices signed the RIBA 2030 Climate Challenge by 2021, about 6% ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2021/07/19/riba-net-zero-carbon-challenge/)), and 1,098 UK firms signed Architects Declare ([UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/36201/html/)). The mature ceiling is NBS, which took decades to reach about 4,000 UK practice customers ([Specification Online](https://specificationonline.co.uk/articles/2019-10-24/nbs-on-track-for-1-000-customers-after-major-investment)). Among contractors, adoption is barely starting: Wates is the first tier one contractor to roll out a reuse platform (Material Index) across its business ([Wates](https://www.wates.co.uk/news/sustainability-services/culture/we-pioneer-nationwide-rollout-of-circular-economy-platform/)). For the launch year, 5 to 15 buyer organisations, 3 to 8 suppliers and 10 to 30 seats give £45,000 to £130,000.

The placeholder prices sit inside the band that comparable tools charge. Madaster UK lists £3,500 to £15,000 a year ([Madaster UK](https://madaster.co.uk/pricing-2/)), One Click LCA charges €3,030 a year per named user and £6,280 for a UK concurrent licence ([One Click LCA UK shop](https://shop.oneclicklca.com/en-gb/building-lca/business-licenses)), Loopfront charges €595 to €995 a month ([Loopfront](https://loopfront.com/product/pricemodel)), and Barbour ABI's construction leads start at £4,000 and average about £7,500 a year ([Barbour ABI](https://barbour-abi.com/?p=11854)). Listing, by contrast, is cheap or free. Globechain charges about £10 per upload ([Cluster Collaboration](http://www.clustercollaboration.eu/content/online-reuse-platform-globechain)), Enviromate takes no commission ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/multi-award-winning-marketplace-start-up-enviromate-relaunches-617153613.html)), and ReLondon's Material Reuse Portal is free ([Material Reuse Portal](https://materialreuseportal.com/)). Two conclusions follow. The £1,200 seat is well below an LCA seat, which leaves room to raise it. The £4,800 supplier fee holds only if it buys compliance outputs, such as audit data and Circular Economy Statement evidence, rather than listing alone.

Europe's subscription universe is far larger on paper. The continent has about **580,000 architects** ([RIBA on the ACE 2024 study](https://www.riba.org/news/new-architects-council-of-europe-ace-2024-sector-study/)), and nearly 70% of practices are solo operations ([EU Build Up](https://build-up.ec.europa.eu/en/resources-and-tools/publications/architects-europe-rising-numbers-gender-balance-and-sustainable)). Germany has 120,175 building architects ([DAB](https://dabonline.de/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/DAB-Mediadaten_Nr52_2025-1.pdf)) and France about 29,500 ([Ministère de la Culture](https://www.culture.gouv.fr/fr/Media/medias-creation-rapide/chiffres-cles-2024_deps_architecture_fiche.pdf)). Institutional owners number under 800: INREV has 516 members and EPRA more than 280 ([EPRA](https://www.epra.com/application/files/7217/7384/9229/EPRA_Annual_Report_March_2026_FV.pdf)). Demolition associations count 829 firms in Germany ([Bundestag lobby register](https://www.lobbyregister.bundestag.de/suche/R003926)) and about 590 demolition and asbestos firms in the Netherlands ([EIB](https://www.eib.nl/pdf/Toekomstperspectieven%20sloopsector%202019_web.pdf)).

Architect seats alone give a **TAM of about £0.7bn**. Scaling the UK SAM by architect headcount gives a Europe-wide SAM of £200m to £350m, but that ignores language, regulation and price. The near-term serviceable part, in France, the Benelux countries and the Nordics, is closer to **£15m to £45m**. France alone has 0.72 times the UK's architect count, which implies £11m to £18m there.

## Europe's serviceable market starts in Paris, not Berlin

France is the one market where regulation already produces the data Tallyard runs on. Since 1 July 2023, demolitions and significant renovations of buildings over 1,000 m2 need a PEMD diagnostic. This is an inventory of products, equipment, materials and waste with reuse options. A post-works form then records what was actually reused, and both are filed on a national platform ([FFB](https://www.ffbatiment.fr/actualites-batiment/actualite/demolition-et-renovation-significative-le-diagnostic-pemd-obligatoire-au-1er-juillet-2023)). That inventory is structurally the same as Tallyard's donor building survey.

The extended producer responsibility scheme for building products (REP PMCB) sets reuse targets rising from 2% in 2024 to 4% to 5% by 2027 and 2028 ([Valobat](https://novabuild.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/VALOBAT-Marine-Webinaire-Novabuild.pdf); [Ecominero](https://www.ecominero.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/cp-ecominero-assises-des-dechets-v1909.pdf)). Actual reuse is at or below 0.2% ([ADEME BatiZoom](https://batizoom.ademe.fr/indicateurs/rep-pmcb-taux-de-recyclage-par-type-de-materiaux)). The scheme tracked only 29,300 t of reuse in 2024, against the 240,000 t ADEME counts across the whole market ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/reemploi-des-materiaux-pourquoi-est-ce-si-complique-dans-le-batiment-277385)). Money is arriving faster than reuse. Eco-organisation budgets went from **€121m in 2023 to €170m in 2024, with about €610m projected for 2027** ([French Senate](https://www.senat.fr/rap/l24-591/l24-5914.html)), and a reform of the scheme is due in 2027. That funding pool is a realistic subsidy route for storage and logistics.

The other markets rank below France. Belgium has the most mature dealer ecosystem: Opalis lists over 300 reclaimed-material companies ([A+ Architecture in Belgium](https://a-plus.be/en/opalis/)). But the market is small. Denmark has the strongest carbon pull. Its whole-life carbon limit fell to an average 7.1 kg CO2e per m2 per year from July 2025, with further cuts in 2027 and 2029 ([The Civil Engineer](https://thecivilengineer.org/news/denmark-phases-in-stricter-co-emissions-rules-for-building-sector)). Finland has required demolition material reports with permit applications since January 2025 ([Interreg Europe](https://www.interregeurope.eu/news-and-events/news/finland-opens-to-consultation-its-building-act)). The Netherlands has high ambition, but sources conflict on whether its building environmental performance limits actually tightened in 2025 ([Binnenlands Bestuur](https://www.binnenlandsbestuur.nl/ruimte-en-milieu/at-osborne/duurzamer-bouwen); [Stimular](https://www.stimular.nl/nieuws/wetgeving-over-duurzaamheid-voor-bedrijven-organisaties-2025)). Germany is the largest construction market at €464bn, but reused products often need case-by-case approval ([Bundestag lobby statement](https://www.lobbyregister.bundestag.de/media/d1/33/500790/Stellungnahme-Gutachten-SG2503310104.pdf)). That keeps it out of the serviceable market until product law changes.

The European materials SAM of **£20m to £60m today and £30m to £100m by 2030** is an assumption. It takes the regulation-led cities (Paris, Brussels, Amsterdam, Copenhagen) to generate two to three times London's core donor flow. France's whole reuse sector, at €80m to €130m across all building types, makes that plausible, but no source measures it directly. The European SOM is nil until Tallyard enters. Opening Paris in 2029, with PEMD filings as the supply feed, would yield £0.5m to £2m of GMV and £0.1m to £0.4m of subscriptions by 2030. Competition there is denser than in London: 10 digital players, Bouygues Construction's Cyneo member network with three technical centres ([L'Usine Nouvelle](https://www.usinenouvelle.com/article/cyneo-la-start-up-de-bouygues-construction-pour-developper-le-reemploi-des-materiaux-du-batiment.N2175852)), Backacia, and Mobius, which reconditions 50,000 to 60,000 m2 of raised floor tiles a year ([Le Flux](https://www.le-flux.fr/paroles-dexperts/parole-dexpert-cout-moindre-gain-carbone-dans-le-batiment-le-reemploi-gagne-du-terrain/)).

## Carbon rules, not waste targets, will move the 2030 numbers

Waste targets do not create reuse demand. London's Circular Economy Statements aim for 95% reuse, recycling or recovery, which can be met entirely by crushing ([GLA guidance](https://www.london.gov.uk/sites/default/files/circular_economy_statements_lpg_0.pdf)). The EU's 70% target was met through backfilling. The rules that move Tallyard's numbers are the ones that price embodied carbon or force an inventory before demolition.

Two effects matter most for the 2030 figures.

The first is that retrofit-first policy cuts both ways. Each scheme that keeps its frame removes donor steel from the market, which suppresses the highest-value commission lots. But it still strips the fit-out layer, and refurbishments made up 66% of central London starts in 2025. Those retrofits also become the main buyers of reclaimed floors, partitions and cladding. The M&S Oxford Street case shows how contested the line remains. The government refused demolition in 2023 on heritage and embodied carbon grounds, the High Court quashed the refusal, and permission was later granted ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/analysis/m-s-decision-nppf-update-embodied-carbon-issue)).

The second is that the UK has no national mandate before about 2028. Outside London, the 2030 SAM rests on voluntary targets. In Europe, the EPBD's 2028 disclosure will spread Danish-style carbon pressure. The SAM growth shown for 2030 assumes the draft London Plan's carbon targets are adopted. If they are not, steel reuse stays near today's 7% to 13% and the UK materials SAM stays nearer £15m to £25m.

The table teaches five lessons. **Matching alone does not pay**: Cycle Up had blue-chip owners and still entered receivership, Backacia needs a 30% list commission and sits inside a group, and the Dutch marketplace Oogstkaart was sold to a deconstruction contractor in 2019 ([Superuse Studios](https://www.superuse-studios.com/?p=52067)). **Processing with a warranty works, at small scale**: Gamle Mursten grew gross profit about 12% a year from 2021 to 2025, while RotorDC's €83,000 per FTE shows labour-heavy, services-like economics. **Software that rides regulation scales**: One Click LCA grew 42% to €20.1m on compliance demand, while reuse-only software stays at about €5m or less. **Contractors and public bodies build their own infrastructure**, from Bouygues's Cyneo and Sweden's not-for-profit CCBuild with over 200 partners ([CCBuild](https://ccbuild.se/en/about-us/organization/)) to London's free Material Reuse Portal, the City-sponsored ROMULUS passport pilot ([NLA](https://nla.london/news/romulus-a-new-circular-economy-initiative)) and the Tipping Point East hub opened in March 2026 ([Dezeen](https://www.dezeen.com/2026/03/27/tipping-point-east-circular-construction-hub-london-yes-make-resolve-collective-material-cultures/)). And **survivors bundle** audits, deconstruction, reconditioning, storage and sales, which matches Tallyard's revenue stack.

On this evidence, Tallyard's UK SOM of £0.5m to £2.1m by 2030 would already make it one of the larger reuse-specific platforms in Europe. Every UK-native rival found has raised under about £1m, and the best-funded adjacent marketplace, Materials Market (new materials, not reuse), raised £2m in May 2025 ([FinSMEs](https://www.finsmes.com/2025/05/materials-market-raises-2m-in-funding.html)). The nearest direct competitor is Material Index, which already uses pre-demolition audits as its front door to brokerage, the same wedge Tallyard targets through surveyors.

## Conclusion

The sizing changes what kind of business Tallyard is. On these numbers it is a compliance-data subscription business with a marketplace attached, not a marketplace that sells subscriptions on the side. The commission line is real, but it is capped by thin discounts to new material and by an 8% take rate below a third of the only published continental benchmark. The subscription line has ten to twenty times the serviceable headroom, and its pricing power comes from what developers must file (audits, Circular Economy Statements, whole-life carbon evidence) rather than from listings that public portals give away. The practical implications follow directly. Price the supplier licence against compliance tools, not listing sites. Treat storage, testing and transport as the margin that makes steel and stone deals clear rather than as minor extras. Test whether 8% should rise for small lots.

The one number to watch is the draft London Plan's upfront carbon target. If adopted on the timetable reported, it is the single decision most likely to lift steel reuse from about 13% towards 30% and roughly double London's serviceable material flow by 2030. If it stalls, Tallyard's growth case rests on voluntary client targets until Part Z or the EPBD's 2028 disclosure arrive. For Europe, the opening is narrower and later than the headcounts suggest: Paris, fed by mandatory PEMD inventories, from about 2029.
